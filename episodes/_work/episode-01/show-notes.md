# Episode 1: Teaching Robots to Feel

<p>For the week of October 16, 2026, Physical AI Weekly explores advances in how robots feel, learn from people, and reach real-world applications. The common thread: promising capabilities still need evidence of operational value.</p><h3>In this episode</h3><ul><li><strong>Daimon puts touch to work</strong>: At IROS 2026, Daimon Robotics demonstrated threading beads onto soft string and securing a magnetic clasp using its Daimon-TWM model and DM-Tac tactile sensors. We explain why contact feedback matters—and why a successful demonstration does not establish production readiness.</li><li><strong>H2L’s fifteen-minute learning claim</strong>: H2L reports 95% task success using fifteen minutes of human experience data captured through body-sensing signals and a simple camera setup. We examine the missing context around task scope, total training effort, and performance under changed conditions.</li><li><strong>TOMO enters the education market</strong>: Axonex AI Group, through Rice Robotics HK, joins Hong Kong’s FLAIR Embodied AI International Applications Hub and launches education-focused humanoid TOMO. The commercial questions center on classroom usefulness, supervision, and whether ecosystem participation leads to paid deployments.</li><li><strong>Conversation meets humanoid robotics</strong>: Cerence and Geely showcase multilingual conversational AI in a humanoid at the Paris Motor Show. We distinguish fluent interaction from reliable physical task completion and consider how bounded service workflows could create value.</li><li><strong>AWS connects the deployment pipeline</strong>: AWS releases an open-source Physical AI toolchain built on NVIDIA’s robotics stack, linking datasets, model development, and policy deployment. We discuss potential integration savings alongside hardware compatibility, security, operating costs, and reliability.</li></ul><p>The executive takeaway: match the technology to a specific bottleneck, and match confidence to the evidence. A bounded pilot should measure success rates, interventions, setup time, and total operating cost—not just the appeal of a headline.</p><h3>What we're watching</h3><ul><li><strong>Repeatable physical performance</strong>: Sustained tactile manipulation results and clearer validation of H2L’s learning claims across tasks and conditions.</li><li><strong>Commercial traction</strong>: Named pilots, measurable classroom or service outcomes, and repeat deployments beyond partnership announcements and demonstrations.</li><li><strong>Deployment economics</strong>: Evidence that open-source tooling reduces integration effort while supporting reliable robot operation.</li></ul>
